# Football as a vehicle for teaching English (age 10–13) and what year 6 in Germany (NRW) expects

Research date: 20 Nov 2026. Target group: German secondary school, year 6 (age 11–12), CEFR A1–A2.
Reading note: "search snippet only" means the claim was seen in a search-result summary, not on the opened page; treat those as leads, not as verified.

## 1. What do existing programmes and materials do with football, and at which level?

### Takeaway
The big football-English programmes (Premier Skills English, Languagecaster) are built for teenage and adult fans at roughly A2–B2 and teach match nouns and commentary idioms, not graded beginner language; the only clearly levelled beginner item found is the British Council LearnEnglish Teens "Football" vocabulary page (A2) with picture matching. German year-5/6 textbooks treat sport as part of "clubs and hobbies / free time", not as a football-commentary topic.

### Cited Findings
- Premier Skills English (British Council + Premier League) is described by the British Council as a vocabulary app with flashcards and quizzes around "the 200 most important words for talking about football"; no CEFR level or grading is stated in the descriptions found. Marketing description, no independent evaluation found. — [British Council Sudan, app page](https://sudan.britishcouncil.org/en/english/children/apps/premier-skills) (search snippet only)
- Premier Skills "200 words" lessons group about 10 words per lesson by theme; examples seen: "game" lesson with advantage, defensive wall, equaliser, header; "match" lesson with kick-off, first half, final whistle, full time, half time, injury time. — [Premier Skills English, 200 words: game](https://premierleague.britishcouncil.org/english/football-vocabulary/200-words/game), [200 words: match](https://premierleague.britishcouncil.org/english/football-vocabulary/200-words/match) (search snippets only)

### Inferences
- Real football commentary vocabulary as taught by Premier Skills/Languagecaster (equaliser, injury time, defensive wall, clichés) is above year-6 level and mostly low-frequency. For A1–A2 the usable core is what LearnEnglish Teens and the textbooks do: concrete, picturable nouns (ball, goal, player, team, pitch) and simple action verbs (kick, pass, run, shoot, score, stop), which also recycle textbook language.
- The common didactic pattern across British Council material is: picture–word matching first, then recognition (multiple choice), then production (gap fill). A game can copy this order.
- Football programmes are marketed on motivation; none of those found publishes learning-gain data for English learners.

### Gaps
- Word lists of Premier Skills' 200 words and of the LearnEnglish Teens football page could not be read (site redirect / words only in PDF).
- Tables of contents for Green Line 1/2, Orange Line 1/2, Camden Town 5/6 and Access 2 were not found; whether any has a dedicated sports unit in year 6 is unverified.
- No Cambridge/Oxford young-learner football material was researched (ran out of search budget). Not verified here: the Cambridge Pre A1 Starters/A1 Movers word lists are commonly said to include sport words such as football, kick, throw, catch, goal.
- No independent evaluation of Arsenal Double Club Languages or Premier Skills English found.

## 2. What do German curricula expect by the end of year 6, and what does CEFR A1/A2 say about listening?

### Takeaway
The NRW Kernlehrplan (Realschule, 2021 text read in full for this section) sets A2 as the target at the end of year 6 and names simple present, present progressive, simple past (plus present perfect, will-future, going to-future), commands and can/must; "Sport" and "Hobbys" are named topics. CEFR places "follow short, simple instructions spoken slowly" at A1 and "catch the main point of short, clear, simple messages and announcements" at A2 — so short commentary lines are a legitimate A1–A2 listening target only if slow, clear and on familiar content.

### Cited Findings
- NRW Realschule, Kernlehrplan Englisch (Erlass 18.06.2021): "Am Ende der Erprobungsstufe erreichen die Schülerinnen und Schüler das Referenzniveau A2 des GeR." — [KLP Englisch Realschule NRW 2021, section 2.2, p. 15](https://lehrplannavigator.nrw.de/system/files/media/document/file/rs_e_klp_2021_07_14.pdf) (PDF read)
- Hör-/Hörsehverstehen, end of year 6: pupils understand "einfache Äußerungen und Hör- bzw. Hörsehtexte ..., wenn diese sich auf vertraute Inhalte beziehen und in einfacher Standardsprache deutlich und langsam gesprochen wird"; they take "wesentliche Informationen" from short classroom contributions, simple everyday conversations and listening texts on familiar content. — [KLP Englisch RS NRW 2021, p. 15](https://lehrplannavigator.nrw.de/system/files/media/document/file/rs_e_klp_2021_07_14.pdf)
- Grammar (fachliche Konkretisierungen, end of year 6): nouns (singular/plural, s-genitive, of-construction); determiners (articles, pronouns, quantifiers); comparison of adjectives; "simple present, present progressive, simple past, present perfect, will-future, going to-future"; modal auxiliaries can/cannot, must; "statements, questions, short answers, negations, commands"; compound sentences (and, or, but), that-clause, adverbial clauses, conditional type 1; word order including "adverbials of place, time, frequency and manner". — [KLP Englisch RS NRW 2021, pp. 17–18](https://lehrplannavigator.nrw.de/system/files/media/document/file/rs_e_klp_2021_07_14.pdf)
- Vocabulary: "einen einfachen Wortschatz produktiv und einen grundlegenden Wortschatz rezeptiv nutzen"; classroom phrases; "einfachen allgemeinen ... sowie thematischen Wortschatz". No word lists, no explicit mention of body parts or prepositions as vocabulary sets. — [KLP Englisch RS NRW 2021, p. 17](https://lehrplannavigator.nrw.de/system/files/media/document/file/rs_e_klp_2021_07_14.pdf)
- Topics (soziokulturelles Orientierungswissen, year 5/6): "Alltag und Freizeitgestaltung von Kindern: Familie, Freunde, Hobbys, Schule, Sport, Reisen, Ernährung, Mediennutzung"; reference country is Great Britain. — [KLP Englisch RS NRW 2021, p. 19](https://lehrplannavigator.nrw.de/system/files/media/document/file/rs_e_klp_2021_07_14.pdf)
- The curriculum states that language means have a "dienende Funktion, die gelingende Kommunikation steht im Vordergrund". — [KLP Englisch RS NRW 2021, p. 17](https://lehrplannavigator.nrw.de/system/files/media/document/file/rs_e_klp_2021_07_14.pdf)
- A separate Kernlehrplan Englisch for Gesamtschule and Sekundarschule was decreed on the same date (18.06.2021, in force for year 5 from 01.08.2021, rising); a 2022 Neufassung for HS/RS/GE/SK added a competence stage after year 8. — [BASS NRW](https://bass.schule.nrw/19443.htm), [Schulministerium NRW 21.06.2022](https://www.schulministerium.nrw/21062022-neufassung-des-kernlehrplans-englisch-hs-rs-ge-sk) (search snippets only; GE text itself not read)
- NRW Gymnasium (G9, KLP 2019): a school curriculum citing it states that pupils reach A2 after the Erprobungsstufe. — [Irmgardis-Gymnasium Köln, schulinterner Lehrplan](https://schule.erzbistum-koeln.de/export/sites/schule/irmgardis-gymnasium-koeln/.content/.galleries/Fachbereiche/Englisch/Englisch_Homepageversion_G9_2_9_22.pdf) (secondary source, search snippet only)
- CEFR, listening to announcements and instructions — A1: "Can understand instructions addressed carefully and slowly to him/her and follow short, simple directions." A2: "Can catch the main point in short, clear, simple messages and announcements." plus understanding simple directions from X to Y. — [CEFtrain, University of Helsinki, quoting Council of Europe scales](https://blogs.helsinki.fi/ceftrain/?p=130) (page opened; wording is that of the 2001 CEFR, edition not stated on the page)
- CEFR overall listening — A1: "Can follow speech which is very slow and carefully articulated, with long pauses for him/her to assimilate meaning." A2: "Can understand enough to be able to meet needs of a concrete type provided speech is clearly and slowly articulated." — [CEFtrain, University of Helsinki](https://blogs.helsinki.fi/ceftrain/?p=130)
- CEFR audio media and recordings — no A1 descriptor; A2: can extract the essential information from short recorded passages on predictable everyday matters delivered slowly and clearly. — [CEFtrain, University of Helsinki](https://blogs.helsinki.fi/ceftrain/?p=130)
- The 2020 Companion Volume keeps the scale under the name "Understanding announcements and instructions"; a 2018 draft adds at A2 "Can understand basic instructions on times, dates and numbers etc., and on routine tasks and assignments to be carried out." — [Council of Europe, Companion Volume 2020](https://rm.coe.int/16809ea0d4), [Companion Volume 2018](https://rm.coe.int/cefr-companion-volume-with-new-descriptors-2018/1680787989) (search snippets only; the Council of Europe server refused direct access)

### Inferences
- A commentary line in a year-6 game fits the curriculum when it is (a) short, (b) slow and clearly spoken in standard English, (c) about content the child can see on screen, and (d) built from simple present / present progressive / simple past and imperatives. Present progressive ("He is running") and simple past ("He scored!") are natural commentary tenses and both are curriculum items; imperatives ("Pass!", "Shoot!") match A1 "follow short, simple directions".
- Real commentary uses simple present for live action ("Kane shoots!"), which pupils learn as the tense for habits. This is not wrong English, but it can clash with what the textbook teaches; a teacher has to decide between authentic simple-present commentary and textbook-conform present progressive.
- Since CEFR has no A1 descriptor for recordings, recorded lines for weaker pupils should behave like A1 instructions: one action per line, long pause, replay possible.
- Body parts and prepositions of place/direction are not listed in the NRW text but fall under "thematischer Wortschatz" and "adverbials of place"; both are standard year-5/6 textbook content (inference, not verified against textbook contents).

### Gaps
- Gesamtschule/Sekundarschule Kernlehrplan text (probably the relevant one for a class with G/E courses) was not read; the direct PDF URL guessed returned 404. Levels there may be differentiated and lower than A2 for part of the pupils — check on the Lehrplannavigator.
- KMK Bildungsstandards (first foreign language, ESA/MSA, 2023 revision) and other states' Bildungspläne were not checked. A Baden-Württemberg example curriculum for year 5/6 surfaced ([schule-bw.de](https://www.schule-bw.de/service-und-tools/bildungsplaene/allgemein-bildende-schulen/bildungsplan-2016/beispielcurricula/sekundarstufe1/BP2016BW_ALLG_SEK1_E1_BC_5-6_BSP_2.docx)) but was not read.
- Exact 2020 Companion Volume wording for Pre-A1/A1/A2 not confirmed from the primary document.

## 3. What does research say (games, sport, topic interest, boys, TPR, chunks)?

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses consistently find medium positive effects of digital games on vocabulary (around d = 0.6–0.7), but with large variation and mostly short studies; topic interest helps comprehension and vocabulary uptake and matters more for boys. Evidence for TPR and for chunk teaching with children points in a positive direction but is methodologically weak; no study was found that tests football specifically as a vehicle for EFL learning.

### Cited Findings
- Kao (2014): meta-analysis of 25 studies on digital game-based learning in EFL contexts, medium positive effect, d = 0.695 (fixed-effect model). — [Kao 2014, Education Journal (CUHK) 42(2), 113–141](https://hkier.cuhk.edu.hk/en/publications/ej-v42n2-113-141) (search snippet only)
- Tsai & Tsai (2018), Computers & Education 125, 345–357: 26 studies (2001–2017). Game vs. other activity (10 studies): large effect. Game with an added/changed feature vs. base game (10 studies): medium. Game vs. same content taught conventionally (only 2 studies): medium to large. Same game, groups differ by a non-game variable (4 studies): not significant. — [Tsai & Tsai 2018, NTNU repository](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/digital-game-based-second-language-vocabulary-learning-and-condit-2/) (abstract page opened; numeric values not shown)
- Yu & Trainin (2022), ReCALL: technology-assisted L2 vocabulary learning in general, 49 effect sizes, overall d = 0.64, with significant heterogeneity. — [Yu & Trainin 2022](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/08A549A6CFD1078406E6A4F8AFE28184/S0958344021000239a.pdf/metaanalysis_examining_technologyassisted_l2_vocabulary_learning.pdf) (search snippet only)
- Özdemir & Seçkin (2024), Quizlet meta-analysis: vocabulary achievement g = 0.62, retention g = 0.74, attitude g = 0.37. — [PMC10951395](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10951395/) (search snippet only)
- Repetition: Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019), Language Learning, meta-analysis: medium correlation between number of encounters and incidental vocabulary learning, r = .34; effect moderated by spacing, visual support and engagement; smaller for primary/secondary pupils than for university students. Earlier single studies proposed thresholds of 6 (Rott 1999), 8 (Horst, Cobb & Meara 1998), 10 (Webb 2007) and 20+ encounters (Waring & Takaki 2003). — [Uchihara et al. summary via UCL repository](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10154988/1/Uchihara%20et%20al.%20%282022%29%20Language%20Learning.pdf), [Cambridge Core citing paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C) (search snippets, secondary citations)
- Topic interest, first-language reading (Germany): Lepper, Stang & McElvany, Reading Research Quarterly (2021/2022), 514 primary pupils: text-based interest and comprehension were related, more strongly for boys; boys' interest depended on topic and protagonist gender (less interest in female-typed topics and female protagonists), girls' interest mainly on text difficulty. Age group: primary, L1 German reading — not EFL year 6. — [TU Dortmund repository](https://eldorado.tu-dortmund.de/handle/2003/40972) (search snippet only)
- Topic interest, EFL: study of 135 Korean EFL students: topic interest raised incidental vocabulary acquisition from reading across proficiency levels; boys acquired fewer words than girls on the low-interest topic text. Older learners than year 6. — [Lee, University of Texas repository](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/46529?show=full) (search snippet only)
- Gender and English in Germany (DESI, year 9, data 2003/04): girls ahead of boys in language competences, lead largest in text production (over 50 points), similar findings for listening; in the speaking test girls led only in vocabulary while boys did better in pronunciation and fluency. No DESI motivation figures found. Year 9, not year 6. — [KMK statement on DESI](https://www.kmk.org/aktuelles-1/artikelansicht/stellungnahme-der-kultusministerkonferenz-zu-den-ergebnissen-der-studie-deutsch-englisch-schuelerleistungen-international-desi.html), [DESI results volume, Beltz 2008](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2010/3149/pdf/978_3_407_25491_7_1A_D_A.pdf) (search snippets only)
- UK study in seven secondary schools (press report): boys wanted more control over content and tasks and often saw no point in foreign-language learning. — [TES, "Girl talk puts male linguists..."](https://tes.com/magazine/archive/girl-talk-puts-male-linguists) (dated press account, search snippet only)
- TPR origin: Asher (1969), "The Total Physical Response approach to second language learning", Modern Language Journal 53, 3–17; Asher's experiments (learners of Russian and Japanese) reported better listening retention when learners acted out commands, with adults outperforming children. — [Asher, ONR report, DTIC](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD0674868.pdf) (search snippet only)
- TPR critique: a re-analysis of Asher's first experiments finds that p < .10 was reported as significant and that the ability test was given after the experiment; it calls for a critical debate on the evidence base. — [Revista Canaria de Estudios Ingleses, Universidad de La Laguna](https://www.ull.es/revistas/index.php/estudios-ingleses/article/view/4353) (search snippet only)
- Newer TPR studies with children are mostly small quasi-experiments with mixed outcomes: a Thai Primary-6 study combined task-based learning + TPR for listening; an Iranian study found the keyword method more effective than TPR for new words in early primary; a 12-week Iranian study (ages 9–10) found no boy/girl difference in vocabulary gain under TPR. — [ThaiTESOL Journal](https://so05.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/thaitesoljournal/article/view/285300), [SAGE Open 2024 via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/aed415c0121141689a3ddb6f0506f686) (search snippets only; effect sizes not retrieved)
- Formulaic language, young learners: a study in Language Teaching for Young Learners found that formulaic sequences helped young EFL learners "improve their fluency, to economize effort on processing and also to buy time for processing". Sample size not retrieved. — [John Benjamins, LTYL](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/ltyl.21006.ghe) (search snippet only)
- Formulaic language, older learners: 120 Chinese EFL learners: frequency of formulaic sequences correlated with speed fluency (r = .563) and breakdown fluency (r = .360). — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.949675) (search snippet only)

### Inferences
- "Games work" is solid at the level of meta-analyses, but the strongest effects come from comparing a game with a different activity; when the same content is taught conventionally the evidence base is tiny (2 studies in Tsai & Tsai). A football game is therefore justified as practice and motivation, not as proof of being better than a good lesson.
- Football as topic: the interest findings support football for those children who like it (in the data, disproportionately boys). The same findings imply a risk: children who are not interested in football gain nothing from the topic. A neutral framing (mixed teams, girls as players and commentators, no fan knowledge needed) limits that risk.
- Listen-and-do is plausible and matches the CEFR A1 descriptor, but the claim "TPR is proven" is not supported by strong studies. In a screen game the "do" is a tap, not a body movement, so TPR research transfers only loosely.
- Commentary is naturally formulaic ("What a goal!", "He shoots – he scores!", "Great save!"). Teaching 8–12 whole phrases as chunks fits the fluency findings and avoids grammar explanation.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on football (or sport) as a topic for EFL learning with outcome data.
- No German year-5/6 data on boys' vs. girls' motivation in English found in this search; DESI is year 9 and 20 years old. Newer IQB Bildungstrend figures were not checked.
- Numeric effect sizes for Tsai & Tsai (2018) and for the child TPR studies were not retrieved.
- Chen, Tseng & Hsiao (2018, BJET) meta-analysis on game-based vocabulary learning was located by title only.

## 4. Proven design principles for small language-learning games

### Takeaway
The best-supported principles are: split input into short learner-paced segments, pre-teach the key words before play, repeat each item many times (spaced, with picture support), and give immediate feedback tied to meaning. Comprehensible-input and differentiation advice is consistent with curriculum and CEFR wording but rests more on professional consensus than on experiments found here.

### Cited Findings
- Segmenting: meta-analysis of 56 investigations (88 comparisons) finds a significant segmenting effect with small-to-medium gains for retention and transfer; segmenting also lowers overall cognitive load and increases learning time. (Authors not shown in the source seen; probably Rey et al. 2019, Educational Psychology Review.) — [doi 10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4) (search snippet only)
- Pre-training principle (Mayer): "people learn more deeply from a multimedia message when they know the names and characteristics of the main concepts". — [Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, chapter on segmenting, pre-training and modality](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/4110A2275F6DCD02DAB1F8B37BA7E5CE) (search snippet only; effect size not confirmed)
- Repetition with support: repetition effect on vocabulary learning (r = .34) is stronger with spaced encounters, visual support and engagement; useful number of encounters reported between 2 and 20. — [Uchihara et al. via UCL repository](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10154988/1/Uchihara%20et%20al.%20%282022%29%20Language%20Learning.pdf) (secondary citation)
- Game features matter: in Tsai & Tsai (2018) adding or changing a single game feature produced a medium effect across 10 studies, i.e. design details change learning outcomes; game design, proficiency and intervention length were tested as moderators. — [Tsai & Tsai 2018](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/digital-game-based-second-language-vocabulary-learning-and-condit-2/)
- Input conditions set by curriculum and CEFR: familiar content, simple standard language, "deutlich und langsam gesprochen" (NRW); "very slow and carefully articulated, with long pauses" (CEFR A1). — [KLP Englisch RS NRW 2021, p. 15](https://lehrplannavigator.nrw.de/system/files/media/document/file/rs_e_klp_2021_07_14.pdf), [CEFtrain Helsinki](https://blogs.helsinki.fi/ceftrain/?p=130)
- Task order used by the British Council for A2 football vocabulary: picture matching → multiple choice → gap fill (recognition before production). — [LearnEnglish Teens](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/vocabulary/a1-a2-vocabulary/football)

### Inferences
Design rules that follow from the findings above (no direct experimental test of this exact combination found):
- One line = one event = one new item. Keep audio lines to roughly 3–7 words, with a pause after each (CEFR A1 "long pauses"; segmenting).
- Pre-teach 6–10 key words with picture and sound before the first match (pre-training principle; picture-first sequence of British Council).
- Plan for at least about 8–10 encounters per word or phrase across several short sessions, in different game situations rather than identical drills (repetition range 2–20, spacing and visual support as moderators).
- Tie each line to something visible on screen at the same moment, so meaning comes from the picture, not from translation (comprehensible input; visual support moderator).
- Listen-and-do: the child's action should prove comprehension ("Pass to the left", "Shoot!"), with feedback within the same second (CEFR A1 "follow short, simple directions").
- Differentiation in two versions of the same line: basic ("He shoots!") and extended ("He shoots with his left foot!"), same core word, so weaker and stronger pupils hear the same vocabulary. This mirrors NRW's distinction between productive "einfacher" and receptive "grundlegender" Wortschatz.
- Avoid overload: no commentary during fast action that also demands a decision; no decorative idioms; replay button; German only as a last-resort help.
- Limit the active set to curriculum language: action verbs in simple present / present progressive / simple past, imperatives, prepositions of place and direction (left, right, into, over, behind), body parts (foot, head, hand), a handful of chunks.

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence found specifically for optimum audio line length for A1–A2 children.
- Immediate vs. delayed feedback in language games: no source retrieved in this search; the recommendation rests on general consensus, not on a cited study.
- Krashen's comprehensible-input hypothesis and Nation's "four strands" were not sourced here.
- Pre-training effect size not confirmed.

## 5. Existing digital games or apps that combine football and English learning for children

### Takeaway
There is no established, independently reviewed football-English game for 10–13-year-old beginners; what exists is either flashcard/quiz vocabulary apps for fans (Premier Skills), school vocabulary apps with a football skin (LinguaLeague), or apps for adult players and fans (FootyLingo, KickOff United). A simple game whose commentary is the learning content appears to fill a real gap.

### Cited Findings
- Premier Skills app (British Council/Premier League): flashcards and quizzes on football words "from actions to players", pitched by British Council country sites at children and teens as well as adults; described as useful for following English commentary. No independent review found. — [British Council Sudan, children's apps](https://sudan.britishcouncil.org/en/english/children/apps/premier-skills), [British Council Tunisia](https://www.britishcouncil.tn/en/english/children/apps/premier-skills) (search snippets only)
- LinguaLeague (Linguascope): for schools and individuals; "twenty foundational vocabulary topics in each language through the medium of football"; rated 4+, in-app purchases. The vocabulary is general topic vocabulary; football is the frame, not the content. — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/td/app/lingualeague/id1480217530) (search snippet only)

### Inferences
- Strength of the existing apps: a clear, limited word set (Premier Skills: 200 words in themed groups of about 10) and picture-supported flashcards. Weakness: language and play are separate — the learner does a quiz about football, but does not understand language in order to play. None of the listings describes listen-and-act mechanics.
- Apps aimed at fans assume football knowledge and teach terminology (offside, equaliser); a class game should assume none.

### Gaps
- None of the apps was installed or tested; all descriptions are store or provider texts (marketing).
- No learning-outcome data or teacher reviews for any of the apps.
- Browser-based classroom games (e.g. football-themed quiz games on ESL sites) were not surveyed.
